In Brazilian Portuguese, country names have grammatical gender. Most countries are treated as either **feminine** (usually with article **a**) or **masculine** (with article **o**). Some are variable or commonly used without articles, but here are common, clear examples.

| Country | Portuguese | Example |
|---|---|---|

| entity["country","Alemanha",""] | a Alemanha | *A Alemanha tem forte indústria.* |
| entity["country","Itália",""] | a Itália | *A Itália é conhecida pela culinária.* |
| entity["country","Argentina",""] | a Argentina | *A Argentina fica na América do Sul.* |
| entity["country","Rússia",""] | a Rússia | *A Rússia é enorme.* |
| entity["country","Índia",""] | a Índia | *A Índia tem grande população.* |
| entity["country","Austrália",""] | a Austrália | *A Austrália tem muitas praias.* |

A rough pattern (not a strict rule):

- Countries ending in **-a** are often feminine:
  - a China, a França, a Rússia
- Many others are masculine:
  - o Brasil, o Japão, o Canadá

But there are exceptions, so native usage matters more than spelling.

# 2. Exceptions to “Countries not ending in -a are masculine”

These countries do **not** end in **-a**, but are usually **feminine**.

| Country | Gender | Example |
|---|---|---|
| entity["country","França",""] | feminine | *A França é bonita.* |
| entity["country","China",""] | feminine | *A China cresce rapidamente.* |
| entity["country","Coreia do Sul",""] | feminine | *A Coreia do Sul é tecnológica.* |
| entity["country","Suíça",""] | feminine | *A Suíça é rica.* |
| entity["country","Grécia",""] | feminine | *A Grécia tem muita história.* |

Important clarification:
- Technically, several above still visually end in “-a” in writing.
- What native learners usually mean by this second “rule” is:
  > “Countries not obviously ending in feminine patterns are often masculine.”
- Better real exceptions for memorization are:

| Country | Why interesting |
|---|---|
| entity["country","Coreia do Sul",""] | ends with “Sul”, still feminine because “Coreia” is feminine |
| entity["country","Costa do Marfim",""] | feminine despite final masculine-looking noun |
| entity["country","Serra Leoa",""] | feminine fixed expression |
| entity["country","Guiné-Bissau",""] | feminine though not ending in simple “-a” |
| entity["country","Nova Zelândia",""] | feminine compound name |

The deeper reality is:

- Portuguese country gender is mostly historical and lexical.
- Endings help, but articles must often simply be memorized:
  - **o Brasil**
  - **a França**
  - **o Canadá**
  - **a China**

But even this category is weak, because in Portuguese:

- most feminine country names still *contain* feminine lexical roots:
  - Coreia
  - Costa
  - Serra
  - Nova

So the truly useful learning insight is actually:

# Better real rule

## Masculine tends to be the “default/unmarked” gender for countries.

Especially:
- short names
- non-Romance foreign names
- names ending in consonants or stressed vowels

Examples:
- o Brasil
- o Japão
- o Peru
- o Chile
- o Canadá

Whereas feminine countries are often:
- inherited from older geographic nouns
- tied to feminine root words:
  - terra
  - costa
  - república
  - ilha
  - nação
  etc.

So honestly, the BEST practical thing is probably memorizing the major feminine countries individually:

- a China
- a França
- a Alemanha
- a Itália
- a Rússia
- a Índia
- a Austrália
- a Argentina

because masculine is otherwise the safer default guess.
